01

Transmittance

How much light gets through

T = transmitted light ÷ incident light
  • Incident = the light going into the filter. Transmitted = the light coming out.
  • Measure both in lux. The units cancel, so T has no units. It can be a decimal or a percent. In lab, use the decimal.
  • Her table: 100 lux in. 75 out → 0.75 · 50 out → 0.5 · 25 out → 0.25.

02

Opacity

How much a filter blocks light

O = 1 ÷ T = incident ÷ transmitted
  • Opacity = the lack of transparency. Her table: T 0.75 → O 1.33 · T 0.5 → O 2 · T 0.25 → O 4.
  • The catch: opacity gets very large very fast. In the classic ink drop experiment (India ink dropped into a beaker of water), each drop doubles the opacity: 2, 4, 8… up to about 1,024 after 10 drops. That's a geometric progression, and hard to graph.
  • Transmittance has the opposite problem: it gets tiny and crowds against zero.
03

Density

The log fixes everything

D = log(opacity) = log(1 ÷ T)
  • Density = how much a substance stops light. "Log" is just a button on your calculator, or =LOG() in Excel.
  • Density turns the exploding opacity into a straight line: 0.3 · 0.6 · 0.9 · 1.2 · 1.5 = 1 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5 stops.
  • Density is additive. Stack a 0.3 and a 0.6 ND filter and you get 0.9 = 3 stops.
  • Graphs: transmittance and opacity curves are almost impossible to read at one end. The density plot is linear, so differences are easy to see.
🔑Darker filter or negative → higher opacity, lower transmittance, higher density.
04

Histograms: a first look

Just counting how often something happens

  • A histogram shows frequency: how many pixels have a value of 150, how many students scored 90+, and so on.
  • An image histogram puts pixel value across the bottom and number of pixels up the side. It tells you nothing about how good the image is.
  • Her kindergarten example: favorite colors decide how many storage bins to buy.

Morning vs afternoon kindergarten

  • Side by side compares two populations. Stacked (cumulative) adds them up: buy 9 red, 8 green, 13 blue, 6 yellow.
  • Always add a legend. Without one, nobody knows which group is which. Your lab plots need one.
