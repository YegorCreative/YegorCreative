01

What you need to make an image

Four ingredients

1 · Subject

Anything from microscopic to a volcano.

2 · Light

No light, no photograph.

3 · Image-forming system

A compound lens, a simple lens, or a pinhole.

4 · Detector

Ground glass, focusing screen, film or a digital sensor.

02

RATS: what happens to light

"Excellent test question" 🎆

★ Her test pick · “Excellent test question”
What 4 things can happen to light when it hits something?
RATS: Reflected · Absorbed · Transmitted · Scattered

R · Reflected

Specular reflection: bounces off at the same angle the other way, like a flashlight on a mirror. Even matte surfaces reflect a little.

A · Absorbed

Taken in and turned into heat. A black shirt gets hotter than a white one.

T · Transmitted

Whatever actually passes through.

S · Scattered

Redirected by dust or water in the air (headlights in fog, wildfire smoke). Causes flare and forms no image.

03

Pinhole vs lens

Without focusing, every pixel gets the same light, so there's no image

Pinhole

  • Can be very sharp, but only at one exact image distance for that pinhole size.
  • Tiny opening, so very long exposures.
  • See pinhole.org.

Lens

  • Collects light much more efficiently.
  • Brings the image to a point of focus.
  • Lets you control depth of field.

The image is upside down and backwards

Every real lens image is inverted. A DSLR flips it back with a mirror and pentaprism. A mirrorless camera flips it in software. A 4×5 camera's ground glass shows it upside down and backwards, so to move the image left you move right.

Pinhole camera: size of the hole

How a DSLR shows the image right-side up

The lens image is upside down and backwards. The mirror sends it up, and the pentaprism flips it right-side up for the optical viewfinder. When you press the shutter, the mirror swings up and light reaches the sensor. Mirrorless cameras flip the image in software instead.

04

Positive vs negative lenses

Converge or diverge

★ Her test pick · Test question
Positive vs negative lens: what does each one do?
Positive (convex): thicker in the center, converges light, forms a real image.
Negative (concave): thinner in the center, diverges light, can't form a real image alone.

➕ Positive (convex)

  • Thicker in the center than at the edges, with at least one convex (outward-curved) side.
  • Makes light converge and forms a real image by itself. It can start a fire with sunlight.
  • Types: double convex, plano-convex (one flat side).

➖ Negative (concave)

  • Thinner in the center, with concave sides.
  • Makes light diverge and cannot form a real image alone.
  • Used with positive lenses. Every camera lens has some, for example the achromatic doublet (a positive and a negative cemented together).

The green ray goes through the optical axis (the center of the lens) undeviated. Rays parallel to it come "from infinity". In the lab, a table's length is far enough.

05

u, v, f and nodal points

The vocabulary of a lens

1/f = 1/u + 1/v
  • u = object distance (subject → lens). v = image distance (lens → image). The focal plane is where the sensor goes.
  • Focal length = the distance from the back nodal point to the focal plane when the object is at infinity.
  • Parallel rays meet at the principal focal point. Light through the front gives the back focal point. Turn the lens around to find the front one.
  • Nodal points: a ray that leaves the lens parallel to how it entered; extend it to the axis to find the front and back nodal points. In a thin simple lens they sit together at the center. In compound lenses they're apart.
🔤U → O: close the top of the U and it becomes an O for Object distance. That leaves V for image distance.

Nodal slide (lab)

Focus a distant bare bulb, then pivot the lens slightly. If the point of light doesn't move, you're on the nodal point, so measure the focal length. If it moves, slide the lens and try again.

Why it matters: for panoramas, rotate the camera on the lens's nodal point (panoramic head) for less distortion when stitching.

Not on the testTwo lenses touching: 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂. With air between them it gets more complicated.

Nodal points: in parallel, out parallel

A ray aimed at the front nodal point leaves as if from the back nodal point, parallel to how it came in. On her nodal slide: pivot the lens, and if the image doesn't move, you're on the nodal point.

Measuring focal length: convex, concave, mirror

F = principal focal point, where parallel rays come into focus. For a negative lens you trace the spreading rays backward. Concave mirrors are used in catadioptric lenses.

    07

    Refraction

    Without it, lenses wouldn't work

    • Refraction = light bending as it passes from one medium into another (unless it hits perpendicular, like on the optical axis).
    • The refractive index says how much a material bends light. Water's is higher than air's, so a straw looks broken.
    • Glass bends light, so lenses can focus. Different wavelengths bend differently (chromatic aberration, next lecture).
    • Short focal length → rays bend sharply → more distortion (wide angle). Long focal length → gentle bending → more magnification, less distortion.

    Her slide: focal length controls…

    1. Magnification (longer = bigger subject) · 2. Angle of view (shorter = wider) · 3. Perspective (relative size of objects). Note: focal length controls angle of view and image scale, but camera position controls perspective.

    • Focal length controls magnification and angle of view (how much of the scene you see, usually measured on the diagonal). Longer lens = bigger subject, less scene.
    • Perspective is controlled by camera-to-subject distance, not by focal length.
    • The lens's image circle must cover the sensor: full frame is 24 × 36 mm, APS-C about 15 × 22.5 mm. Bigger glass = pricier lens. A full-frame lens on a crop body uses the sharp center ("sweet spot").
