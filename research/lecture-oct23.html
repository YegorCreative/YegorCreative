01

Anamorphic lenses

A "funhouse mirror" in a lens

An anamorphic lens squeezes a wide scene sideways so it fits on standard motion-picture film. A second anamorphic lens on the projector stretches it back out, so the audience sees a wide, undistorted picture.

Squeeze, then stretch

  • The camera lens and the projector lens work together (one squeezes, one un-squeezes).
  • Motion-picture film is a positive film, like slide film.
  • Digitally, software (e.g. Premiere Pro) can de-squeeze the footage for you.
  • Why bother? It lets a wide picture use standard film, so it can play in any theater.
🔑Memory hook: funhouse mirror. One makes you tall and skinny (on the film), the other makes you short and wide again (on the screen).
02

Bokeh

The quality of the blur

Bokeh = the quality of the blur in out-of-focus areas
  • Determined by lens aberrations and the aperture.
  • Left alone, bokeh usually takes the shape of your aperture (the blades).
  • Important with lenses that give a shallow depth of field: telephoto and macro.
  • You can put a cutout over the lens to give bokeh a shape (hearts, stars), and even hide information in it.

Change the aperture shape

Out-of-focus points of light copy the shape of the opening.

03

Chromatic aberration

Colors don't focus at the same point

All wavelengths of light do not bend the same amount. Short wavelengths (blue) bend the most, long wavelengths (red) the least, and green is in between. The result is color fringing around edges.

Single lens vs achromatic doublet

Where does the sensor go?

Where green comes to focus. Green has the most pixels (Bayer: 50%) and is where our vision is most sensitive.

How lenses fight it

  • Achromatic doublet: a positive (crown glass) and a negative (flint glass) lens cemented together. That's why every lens has a negative element.
  • The aperture cuts off the outer rays, which bend the most.
  • Coatings on the lens.

Nobody wants chromatic aberration. Your eyes have it too, but your pupil cuts off the outer rays. With fully dilated pupils (like at the eye doctor) you can see blue fringes.

🔑Blue Bends Best. Red bends least. Green sits in the middle, and that's where the sensor goes.
04

Spherical aberration

Edge rays and center rays focus in different places

Spherical aberration: the rays passing through a lens come to focus at different points. Rays through the edge of the lens focus closer than rays through the center, so the image is unsharp.

  • No lens is perfect. A perfect, affordable one doesn't exist yet.
  • It mostly comes down to the quality of the optics: you see more of it in cheaper lenses, and it can't really be corrected later.
  • A soft-focus lens under-corrects it on purpose (Oct 19).
05

Distortion: barrel & pincushion

Straight lines that bend

Bend the grid

Left = barrel · middle = none · right = pincushion

Barrel

  • Looks like a barrel: the edges bulge out.
  • Magnification decreases with distance from the optical axis.
  • Seen in fisheye and wide-angle lenses.

Pincushion

  • Named after a sewing pincushion: lines curve in.
  • Magnification increases away from the axis.
  • Negative lenses (glasses for nearsighted people) do this: it can look like a "chunk of the head" is missing.
  • Fix it in Lightroom → Profile Corrections. Your lens is in the metadata, and Lightroom has the manufacturer's lens models built in.
  • Fixing distortion means creating pixels, so you can get artifacts.
  • Vignetting (dark corners) is easy to correct digitally.

Image properties & the perfect pinhole

There's one best hole size

  • The image is real: it can be captured on a screen, sensor or film.
  • Light travels in straight lines.
  • The image is inverted and laterally reversed (upside down and backwards). You'll see it on the 4×5 ground glass.
D = √f ÷ 141  (inches)
D = √f ÷ 28  (millimeters)

D = pinhole diameter · f = pinhole-to-sensor (or film) distance, in the same unit

Pinhole calculator

    • Hole too big → more light, but a blurry image.
    • Hole too small → less light, and blur from diffraction.
    • Fun facts: pinhole.org runs a Worldwide Pinhole Photography Day. Students once turned a whole dorm room into a pinhole camera by measuring window → wall distance and using this formula.

    Diffraction

    Why f/22 isn't always a good idea

    When light passes through a small hole (a pinhole or a tiny aperture), it disperses, or diffracts. A point becomes a bright center with faint rings around it.

    Laser through a pinhole: a bright center + rings

    • Usually negligible, but it increases for very small apertures.
    • The dispersion softens the image.
    • When that softening cancels out the sharpness you gain from stopping down, you've reached the lens's diffraction limit.
    • Every lens has a sweet spot: learn where yours is.

    08

    Depth of field & the circle of confusion

    "Depth of field is a lie"

    Depth of field = the range of distances within which objects are imaged with acceptable sharpness. In theory you can only focus on one distance. Everything else makes a small circle instead of a point, but your eye has a limited resolving power, so small circles still look like points.

    Permissible circle of confusion = the largest circle that still appears as a point

    Move the object and watch its blur circle

    ★ Her test pick · "Beautiful test question"
    What affects your circle of confusion?
    1. Individual eyesight
    2. Contrast between the circle and the background
    3. Level of illumination
    4. The viewer's opinion of what is sharp (subjective)
    🔑"Every Coffee Looks Okay": Eyesight · Contrast · Light level · Opinion.

    Low contrast and dim light make blur harder to see, so things seem sharper. That's why it's easier to read with the light on.

    09

    Depth of field controls & relative f-stops

    Three dials for sharpness front to back

    ★ Her test pick · "Also a beautiful test question"
    What controls depth of field?
    1. Aperture
    2. Object distance (camera to subject)
    3. Focal length
    To get MORE depth of fieldTo get LESS (blurry background)
    Smaller aperture (f/22, f/32)Wider aperture (f/2.8, f/5.6)
    Move farther from the subjectMove closer
    Shorter (wider) focal lengthLonger (telephoto) focal length

    Her slides: daffodils at f/32 (background readable) vs f/5.6 (background melts away). Her zoo armadillo: long lens, wide open, so the bleachers behind it turned into a soft blur. Depth of field made the photo.

    Relative f-stops: same f-number, different hole

    f-number = focal length ÷ aperture diameter

    f/5.6 is a different physical size on a 50 mm lens than on a 300 mm lens, but lets in the same amount of light. The same f-stop doesn't give the same depth of field on every lens, so learn your lenses.

    10

    Hyperfocal distance

    The landscape photographer's trick

    Hyperfocal distance is defined as:

    1. the closest distance that appears sharp when the lens is focused at infinity, or
    2. the closest distance you can focus on and still have an object at infinity appear sharp.
    Focus at the hyperfocal distance → sharp from ½ of it to infinity

    Try it

    Using the depth of field scale (her slide)

    • The red line = where you're focused. The pairs of numbers (4, 11, 16, 22) on either side = the depth of field at each f-stop.
    • At f/22, focused at ∞: the DOF scale shows sharp from 20 ft to infinity. So 20 ft is the hyperfocal distance.
    • Turn the focus ring so ∞ sits on the other f/22 mark: now it's sharp from about 13 ft to infinity.
    • That's an increase of 7 ft in depth of field, for free.

    Her examples: a fence and house, a cactus with mountains, ice on a shoreline with mountains. Focus on the foreground and the background blurs; focus at ∞ and the foreground blurs; focus at the hyperfocal distance and both are sharp.

    Beyond the lectureThe simulator uses H = f² ÷ (N × c) + f with c = 0.03 mm (full frame). Her lens scale is a different lens, so its numbers differ. In theory focusing at H gives sharpness from H ÷ 2; her scale reading gave ~13 ft. Use her numbers on the exam.
    11

    Handheld shutter rule & image stabilization

    What you did in Lab 2

    slowest handheld shutter = 1 ÷ (focal length × crop factor)
    • Full frame, 100 mm lens → 1/100 (crop factor of full frame = 1).
    • The rule was made before image stabilization. IS lets you go slower.
    • Your limit changes with age, fatigue (the end of a soccer game!) and the camera's weight and balance. That's why the lab had you test yours with IS off, then on.
    ⚠ She stressed this
    Should IS be on when the camera is on a tripod?
    No, turn it off. Mechanical stabilization (lens or body) assumes there's movement and adds motion and wobble. It can damage the sensor, camera or lens. Handholding → turn it on.
