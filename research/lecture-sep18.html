02

Stops and the full-stop table

"Free give-me points"

★ Her test pick · "You need to have this memorized for the test"
Know every full stop for aperture, shutter speed and ISO.
"I can guarantee you, you'll get a lot of points if you know this table. I guarantee you, you will miss a lot of points if you don't."

An exposure needs three settings: shutter speed, aperture (f-number) and ISO. A stop is one full step in any of them, and it halves or doubles the exposure.

SettingFull stops (each step = ×2 or ÷2 the light)
f-number1.4 · 2 · 2.8 · 4 · 5.6 · 8 · 11 · 16 · 22 · 32
Shutter (s)1 · 1/2 · 1/4 · 1/8 · 1/15 · 1/30 · 1/60 · 1/125 · 1/250 · 1/500 · 1/1000

🔑f-numbers: two rows that each double: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 1.4, 2.8, 5.6, 11, 22. Interleave them. Bigger f-number = smaller opening = less light.
03

Equivalent exposures

Many ways to make a dollar

★ Her test pick · on every test
"We will have these on every test, this semester and next semester."
"Been doing this for 22 years. I've never had a test without one."
  • Four quarters, ten dimes, twenty nickels: all make a dollar. Many combinations of settings make the same exposure too.
  • Bucket analogy: a small hose for a long time or a big hose for a short time both fill the bucket (a proper exposure).
  • If you change one setting, you must change at least one other.

Her method

  1. Write the base exposure N.
  2. For each change, count the stops. + = adds light, − = takes light away.
  3. The column must add up to 0.

04

What each setting does to the picture

The numbers matter less than the effect

Shutter speed

Freezes motion or blurs motion (a waterfall, a swinging bat). Motion blur ≠ a blurry image everywhere, which is camera shake.

Aperture

Very shallow to very deep depth of field. Her dewdrop leaf: deep DOF shows every drop, shallow DOF picks a few.

ISO

Higher ISO = more noise (digital). With film, say graininess. Mixing them up makes you "look uneducated".

  • With enough light, the right equipment and patience, you can get all three the way you want.
  • Check that the exposure compensation marker on your camera is in the center. If it isn't, compensation is on.
05

Reading a histogram

It tells you about exposure, not quality

★ Her test pick · "Write that down and underline it"
How many digital counts does the camera histogram have?
256, numbered 0 to 255. Most people say 255, but zero counts.
  • The in-camera histogram comes from a JPEG, even when you shoot RAW. It's close, but RAW software may show a slightly different histogram.
  • 0 = as dark as it gets. 255 = as bright as it gets. Split it into thirds: shadows · midtones · highlights.
  • There's no single correct histogram. Midtones only = low dynamic range, which is right if that's the scene (her foggy Venice Beach photo) and wrong if you expected highlights and shadows.
  • Clipping = pixels piled at 0 or 255. Clipped detail is gone, RAW or JPEG. Specular highlights (sparkles on raindrops or tomatoes) should clip, or they look cloudy.
  • Full dynamic range = values from end to end (her rose). A bright moon over a dark ski slope clips both ends: a perfect candidate for HDR.
  • Photoshop and Lightroom: hold Option/Alt while dragging a Levels slider to see which pixels clip.
  • Too bright with flash up close? Back up (inverse square law). That's what she did at the Monkey Jungle.
06

ETTR and N+2 / N−2 tables

Purposely over- and underexposing

  • Exposure to the right (ETTR): for a dark, low-dynamic-range subject (her black dog), overexpose without clipping, then pull it back in post for more shadow detail. More on this in the Sep 21 and Sep 28 tabs.
  • Lab tables say N + 2 or N − 2: always count from the base exposure on the top row, never from the row above.
  • These are not equivalent exposures. N + 2 is deliberately 2 stops over, N − 2 is 2 stops under.
  • Labs use JPEG so you see your mistakes (like a "daylight" LED panel that's really about 5,200 K). Switch back to RAW for Photo 1.
