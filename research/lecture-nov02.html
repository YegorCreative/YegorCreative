01

Change focal length AND distance together

Keep the near object the same size and watch the background

Her boots again. This time she kept the near boot the same size: double the focal length, double the distance.

Her boot series

  • The boots never moved, but with the longer lens from farther away they look closer in size and closer together.
  • Long lens from far away = background looks big and close (emphasized). Wide lens up close = background looks small and far (de-emphasized).
  • Real-world use: a real-estate shot can shrink the run-down house behind, or make a park 2 miles away look next door. A TV reporter's hotel looked right next to the Capitol (it isn't). Her lesson: "There's no truth in imagery." Check Google Earth before you book.
  • Words of caution: the exact equations assume the lens-to-sensor distance stays constant. It doesn't (you refocus, zoom lenses change), so she cares that you understand what happens, not that you calculate it.
02

Weak vs strong (linear) perspective

It's the distance, not the lens

Weak perspective

  • Camera farther away
  • Space looks compressed
  • Parallel lines stay close to parallel
  • Size drops slowly with distance
  • Usually linked to a telephoto

Strong perspective

  • Camera closer
  • Near object grows faster than the far one
  • Space between them looks bigger
  • Parallel lines converge
  • Usually linked to a wide angle
⚠️The trap: strong perspective isn't caused by the wide-angle lens. You use a wide angle because you're close, and being close causes it. Same for telephoto: you're far away. "It's not a function of the lens, it's a function of that distance."
03

Viewing distance

How far away to look at a print so the perspective looks normal

viewing distance = focal length × printing magnification

Try it

  • 8×10 camera, 12-inch lens, contact print (no enlargement, ×1) → view from 12 in. Nobody holds a print that close.
  • Full frame, 50 mm lens, 8× enlargement → 50 × 8 = 400 mm ≈ 16 in. (In class she said "about 10 inches". The math gives about 16 in. 10 in would be a 5× enlargement. Ask her or SI which answer she wants.)
  • People usually stand about 3–4 ft from a print on a gallery wall.
  • The velvet rope in museums is not about viewing distance. It keeps your "grubby paws" off the art.
04

More depth cues

How a flat photo still shows depth

CueHer example and what it tells you
TextureSand-dune ripples from above, a palm-tree trunk. Texture gets finer with distance.
FormHot-air balloons: you know they're 3-D and roughly how big, so a smaller one must be farther.
Size (known objects)A person next to pillars tells you how tall the pillars are. The moon looks huge near the horizon next to a mountain.
Size tricksHer cathedral: windows get smaller going up, so the building looks taller. Disney and movie sets use this.
Aerial hazeHaze lowers saturation and contrast and raises brightness with distance.
OverlapMarching soldiers get smaller down the line. The camels look bigger than the pyramids.
ShadowsJewelry with no shadow is hard to size. A little shadow gives shape and scale.
IsolationAn object alone (a bowl or a contact lens? a marble or a bowling ball?) has no scale. Add a known object and you can tell.
Approaching vs receding colorsWarm colors (reds) seem to come forward. Cool colors (blues, deep greens) seem to recede. In advertising, don't put the star in a receding color surrounded by approaching colors.

Why warm colors come forward is biology (eye anatomy), which she'll explain next semester.

05

Holography & stereo images

"Cheating" ways to show 3-D

  • Holography was invented by Dennis Gabor, a Hungarian physicist who won the Nobel Prize in Physics. He wasn't trying to make holograms. He was trying to improve the resolving power of electron microscopes, and by accident invented electron holography.
  • His early holograms had flaws because a good hologram needs a coherent light source: a laser.
  • Hologram = one high-resolution photograph that shows different images to the left and right eye (like a stereo pair). Move it sideways and you get motion parallax, so the image seems to move.
  • Stereo images: two side-by-side views. Relax your eyes until you see three blurry images, then let the middle one come into focus to see 3-D. Image analysts in intelligence read stereo pairs all day.
  • Sit near the center in lecture: the screen is retroreflective, so the image gets dimmer off-axis.
06

Depth of field again, plus vignetting

Recap and a student question

  • Depth of field = the range of distances that look acceptably sharp. Only one plane is truly in focus. Points nearby make tiny circles of confusion your eye can't tell from dots.
  • Her examples: f/1.8 vs f/5.6, and focusing on the foreground, middle ground or background. Her armadillo photo used very shallow depth of field to hide the bleachers behind it.
  • Vignetting (darker corners) follows cosine falloff and changes with f-number. Test it: photograph a transparency illuminator and change the f-number. Software can remove it with a lens profile. Some photographers even add it on purpose.
