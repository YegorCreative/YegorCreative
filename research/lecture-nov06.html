01

Review terms

Words you need for filter math

TermMeaning
Pixel valueHow bright a pixel is. In a color file, how bright its red, green or blue is.
Bit depthThe number of bits needed for all the possible brightness levels (8-bit = 256 levels).
Pixel addressThe row and column where a pixel sits.
IntegerA whole number: 1, 2, 3… Image files are made of integers.
Floating pointA number with a decimal part: 0.175, 23.726. Filter math makes these, so you round back to an integer.
  • A digital image is a 2-D array of numbers. The sensor is linear (more light = more voltage), but the voltage must be binned into whole pixel values, which is why digital edges stair-step and film edges are smooth.
02

Pixel address

Row and column, but which comes first?

  • Her example pixel is in the 9th row and 16th column.
  • Row major writes the row first: (9, 16). Column major writes the column first: (16, 9).
  • Where (0, 0) is, and whether counting starts at 0 or 1, is software dependent: it's whatever the programmers chose. In her software, (0, 0) is the top left.
03

Resizing & interpolation

Making up pixels that weren't there

  • Downsizing: throw pixels away. Upsizing: copy pixels, or create new in-between values.
  • Interpolation = using known data to estimate unknown data. You've seen it before in demosaicing (RAW → full RGB), and it's used for resizing in both directions.
  • Straight-line data is easy to interpolate. Curved data needs a higher-order (squared or cubed) equation.
MethodHow it works and when it fails
Nearest neighborThe simplest. Halving throws out every other pixel in every other row, so a checkerboard can turn into a solid color. Doubling makes each pixel 2, so it just gets blocky. "Not the one we really ever use."
BilinearLinear in both directions, using the pixel above, below, left and right. Good for photos, but black text turns gray (less contrast and sharpness).
BicubicUses a cubic (third-power) equation and many more neighbors. Best for pictorial images. Bicubic smoother for enlarging, bicubic sharper for reducing.

She said bicubic uses "15 of the closest neighbors". Most references say 16 (a 4 × 4 block). If it shows up on a test, ask her which number she wants.

04

Pixel point vs pixel group processing

"I can guarantee you're going to have to define these on a test"

★ Her test pick · "Guarantee" you'll define these
What is pixel point processing, and what is pixel group processing?
Point: changes one pixel without looking at its neighbors.
Group: changes the center pixel using the pixels around it.

Point

  • Fixing a hot or bad pixel by hand
  • A dust speck
  • Her example: a green bad pixel in a pupil's highlight. Group processing would turn it gray, so fix just that pixel.

Group

  • Scaling (resizing)
  • Altering scene details
  • All spatial filters: blur, sharpen, edge finding
🔑Point = one pixel alone. Group = the pixel and its neighbors.
05

Spatial filters

A small kernel slides over the image

  • A spatial filter (kernel) works on the center pixel and uses its neighbors to calculate the new value.
  • Kernels are small and always odd: 3 × 3, 5 × 5, 9 × 9, so there's a true center.
  • Filters always change brightness trends. They can blur, sharpen (really an illusion of sharpness), find features (like "select sky") and make special effects. All of it comes down to finding edges.
  • Blurring = making the difference between neighboring pixels smaller (255 next to 128 → both move toward each other). A big difference looks sharp. Use it to soften a background, or on scans of old halftone magazines.
  • Real software works in the frequency domain (Fourier transform). She teaches the spatial way, which gives the same result.
06

Filter math

Multiply, add, round, slide

  1. Lay the 3 × 3 filter (F) on top of the image (P), centered on the pixel you want.
  2. Multiply each filter value by the pixel under it: F1 × P1, F2 × P2… F9 × P9.
  3. Add all nine products.
  4. Round to a whole number. That's the new center value.
  5. Slide one pixel over and repeat. The filter values never change. Skip the edge pixels for now.

Try it: tap a pixel

Image (her letter T)

Filter

  • Blurring kernel rules: all coefficients are positive, and they add up to 1 (so overall brightness stays the same).
  • Her example: eight 255s and one 0 under a 1/9 filter → (8 × 255 + 0) ÷ 9 = 226.67 → 227. It dropped because it picked up a bit of the black.
  • In Excel: type the first formula with cell references (not numbers), then click-drag across the row and down. Use the decimal buttons to show whole numbers.
  • Many programs skip tiny differences (e.g. less than 5 counts = texture) and only blur real edges.
