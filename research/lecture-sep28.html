01

Bayer pattern & demosaicing

Each photosite sees only one color

A sensor only measures brightness. To get color, a Bayer filter (color filter array, CFA) puts a red, green or blue filter over each photosite: 50% green, 25% red, 25% blue. So every pixel is missing two thirds of its color data. Demosaicing is the processing that fills in the missing colors from the neighbors.

How the standard method works

  • Green first: most data (50%) and where we're most sensitive, so it builds the luminance (brightness) image.
  • Then red and blue become the chrominance (color) images. Blue gets the biggest boost, so blue has the most noise.
  • Good algorithms look for edges and weight pixels along the edge more.

Why white needs all three

On white areas, the red, green and blue photosites are all "on". White = all colors of light together.

Armenian: demosaicing ≈ գույների վերականգնում (rebuilding colors), photosite = լուսազգայուն բջիջ.

02

Other color filter arrays

Bayer isn't the only pattern

No filter Foveon X3 (Sigma)

  • A direct image sensor: records red, green and blue at every photosite, using how deep each color goes into the silicon. Like color film's 3 layers.
  • Pros: no demosaicing (no interpolation), sharper still lifes, less processing.
  • Cons: blur on moving subjects, and "funky" colors under some lights.
  • Sigma bought Foveon in 2008, and it's the only company using it. No camera currently in production.
03

ISO is gain, and noise is not grain

You can't change how sensitive the sensor is

The photodiode's sensitivity is fixed. Raising ISO just amplifies the signal (more gain), and the noise gets amplified with it.

Low ISO

Longer exposure time for the same light, less noise.

High ISO

Shorter exposure time, more noise. Having a high ISO available doesn't mean you'll like using it.

Film Grain

Real silver halide crystals (or dye particles). Faster film = bigger crystals = a bigger target for photons = visible grain.

Digital Noise

Electronic noise. It has nothing to do with crystals. Don't call digital noise "grain".

Beyond the lectureThe professor says ISO = "International Standards Organization", so use her wording on the exam. The official English name is the International Organization for Standardization, and "ISO" comes from Greek isos = equal.
04

Three types of noise

Noise = a mismatch between the light that hits the sensor and the electronic signal

Her slide quotes Galer & Horvat (Digital Imaging Essential Skills): noise comes from random electrical activity on the sensor, and the lower the light, the greater the inconsistency.

Most common

Photon noise

Light itself isn't perfectly even. Photons arrive randomly, so the input is already noisy.

Like rain: even steady rain doesn't drop the same number of drops in every cup.

Heat

Thermal noise

Heat from the camera's electronics makes the photosite "see" fake photons. Fix: cooled sensors (used for IR and science cameras).

Hotter camera, long exposures → more thermal noise.

Readout

Transfer noise

Happens during readout: some charge gets left behind, or neighboring photosites interfere (like blooming).

Like pouring syrup from a can: you need a scraper to get the rest.

💡P-T-T: "Please Turn on the Tripod." Photon, Thermal, Transfer. Photon noise comes from the light, thermal from heat, transfer from readout.
05

Signal-to-noise ratio

How strong the signal is compared to the noise

SNR = 20 × log₁₀(Mean ÷ Std Dev)
  • Higher SNR = better (cleaner). A low standard deviation is what you want: less spread around the mean.
  • Units are dB (decibels: small d, capital B), borrowed from audio. On a lab answer the dB is often left off, but it is a dB.
  • SNR doesn't depend on amplification: if the mean and std dev are both multiplied by the same gain, the ratio stays the same.

06

Sensor size

Bigger photosite = bigger target for photons

  • Larger photosite → more light-gathering ability → less amplification needed → less noise. Crop sensors tend to have more noise.
  • More photosites (a larger array) → higher resolution.
  • A bigger sensor needs bigger, heavier, pricier lenses, and only a few full-frame sensors fit on one silicon wafer, so they cost more.
  • Pixel density is not tied to ISO.
  • Her target users: full frame → professionals; APS-C → professionals and "prosumers"; Four Thirds → "???"; anything smaller → consumer.
🏓Ping-pong ball test: throw it at a small bucket or a big barrel. You hit the barrel more often. Bigger photosite = more photons caught.
Beyond the lectureIn class, Four Thirds was called "about 30–40% smaller" than full frame. Measured by area it's actually about 74% smaller (crop factor 2 → ¼ the area). If the exam asks, use the class answer, but now you know the real math: area shrinks by the crop factor squared.
07

Expose to the right (ETTR)

Move dark tones up, away from the noise

A raw sensor responds linearly: the brightest stop gets half of all the levels, the next stop half of what's left, and so on. Shadows get very few levels and sit close to the noise. ETTR means exposing brighter so the histogram moves right (without clipping), then darkening it again in post.

Stop (12-bit raw)Levels

✅ Works for

Low-key and mid-to-dark scenes that don't fill the whole dynamic range.

❌ Not for

Scenes with bright highlights and dark shadows: a white cat and a black cat, a bride and a groom, a bright day with clouds. The bright parts would clip.

📸Rules: shoot raw (JPEGs aren't linear and throw data away), watch the luminance histogram, and never clip what matters.
08

HDR (high dynamic range)

When the scene is too contrasty for one shot

  • Camera on a sturdy tripod (if it moves, the merge fails). Shoot several exposures: one for the highlights, one for the shadows, maybe more in between.
  • Merge in Photoshop or Lightroom (or dedicated tools). Don't "overbake" it.
  • Used for: architecture and real estate (a room plus the view through the windows), night venues with bright lights, sunny scenes with deep shadows.
⚖️ETTR vs HDR: ETTR = one shot, for scenes that fit the sensor. HDR = many shots, for scenes that don't fit.
