The 4×5 view camera

A lens, a bellows and a ground glass

Tap a part

  • Movements: the standards slide forward and back, tilt, swing (sideways) and rise/fall. That's why it's "the ultimate machine for architecture": it corrects distortion that we now usually fix in Lightroom.
  • To focus, the shutter must be open so light reaches the ground glass. Slide the front or rear standard, check with a magnifier, and put a focusing cloth (a jacket works) over your head because the image is dim.
  • The image on the ground glass is upside down and backwards.
  • Changing the lens: grip the lens firmly, press the release button and slide it out. It's keyed, so it only goes back one way. Give it a tug to check it's locked.
  • Close the shutter, insert the film holder, pull the dark slide, fire with a cable release (no shake), put the slide back.
  • 4×5 film comes in single sheets with notches (to find the emulsion side in the dark) and is loaded in a darkroom. A holder takes two shots. Her rule: flip the dark slide so you can tell which sheets are already exposed.
02

Pinholes and the optimal pinhole size

Too big = blur, too small = diffraction

In the lab a pinhole "lens" (no glass, just holes of different sizes) is taped onto a 4×5. One partner looks at the ground glass while the other changes the hole size, at two image distances: bellows fully closed and bellows fully extended. You'll find the sharpest hole is not the same for both, and the tiniest hole isn't always best.

D = √f ÷ 28 (mm)  ·  D = √f ÷ 141 (inches)
f = pinhole-to-ground-glass distance

Pinhole simulator

    🔑Longer bellows → bigger best hole. The best size grows with the square root of the distance. Each hole also has an f-number: N = distance ÷ hole (pinholes are around f/150 to f/700).
    Beyond the labThe simulator adds two blurs: the hole's own size (geometric blur) and diffraction (about 2.44 × wavelength × f-number). The smallest total blur lands almost exactly on √f ÷ 28, which is where that formula comes from.

    • Stopping down increases depth of field: the top of the bear's head and the wall targets get sharper.

    Part II: 1:1 and 2:1 reproduction

    Calculate first, then set the camera

    v = f (m + 1)  ·  m = v ÷ u  →  u = v ÷ m

    Distance calculator (practice lens)

      Fill in the lab table (practice numbers)

      • Object distance u: from the lens's center line (about the front nodal point) to the arrow on the light box. Image distance v: from the lens to where the film sits (the ground-glass ridge).
      • Lock the front standard at u, slide the rear standard to v, then make small focus adjustments. Measure the object and image the same way (side to side).
      • Why it's not perfect: the true nodal points of the recessed lens are only estimated, the ground glass is hard to measure exactly, and focusing by eye isn't precise.
      • At 1:1, u = v, and both equal 2 × the focal length. At 2:1, v = 3f and u = 1.5f.
      • Why it matters: advertising and biomedical work need exact sizes (a ring for a jeweler, or the "enlarged to show texture" cereal box).
      05

      Part III: finding an unknown focal length

      No pivot, no infinity: use what you know

      The lens is taped so you can't read it and locked so you can't pivot it, and the light isn't at infinity. The front and rear nodal points are marked. You have a light box, a focusing screen and a ruler. Practice on this made-up lens: move the light and the screen until the image is sharp, then work out f.

      Practice optical bench

      Method 1 · Lens equation

      Get it sharp, measure u and v from the nodal points: 1/f = 1/u + 1/v (press 1/x!).

      Method 2 · Make it 1:1

      Move both until the image is the same size as the object. Then u = v = 2f, so f = u ÷ 2.

      Method 3 · Magnification

      Measure image ÷ object = m and v: f = v ÷ (m + 1).

      Why learn this? Some lenses have no focal length marked, or it's been rubbed off. The lab says not to share your own lab solution with other groups.

      Part IV: seeing chromatic aberration

      Defocus a little each way and look at the edge

      Focus on the black square of the resolution target with a magnifier, then shorten the bellows a little and lengthen it a little. Do it with the single uncoated lens and the coated achromatic lens.

      Try it

      Defocused by…Single lensCompound (achromatic) lens
      Reducing the bellows (shorter)BlueNo hue
      Increasing the bellows (longer)RedNo hue
      • Blue bends most, so it focuses closest to the lens: shorten the bellows and blue comes into focus first. Red focuses farthest (past green): lengthen the bellows to see it.
      • The sensor or film normally sits where green focuses.
      • The expensive coated, achromatic lens shows no colors. Coatings and achromatic doublets fix it, the same reason eyeglasses get coatings. Very strong glasses (±5 to 7 diopters) can still show color at the edges.
      • Don't defocus a lot: way out of focus you see every color, which doesn't count.
      Beyond the labWhat you actually see at the edge depends on the blurred colors around the sharp one, so classmates sometimes describe it differently. Her rule for the table: shorter bellows → the color that focuses first; longer → the color that focuses last.
