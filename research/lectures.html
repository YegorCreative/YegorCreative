01

How a digital camera works

A film camera with a sensor where the film was

  • Same lens, shutter and aperture as a film camera. The sensor replaced the film and translates the optical image into an electrical signal.
  • The ⦶ mark on top of your camera (a circle with a line through it) shows where the sensor (film plane) sits. It's handy for measuring image distance.
  • A sensor is a 2-D array of photodiodes (photosites). Each one collects photons and builds up a charge.

Photosite

On the sensor. It catches light.

Pixel

In the file. It's the number the photosite becomes after processing.

🎨Photosites are colorblind. A photon is a photon. They only "see" brightness, which is why we need a color filter array.
02

Analog → digital (A/D conversion)

Smooth voltage becomes steps

  • More light → more voltage. Voltage is analog: unlimited levels (1.375647 V is fine).
  • The analog-to-digital (A/D) converter sorts each voltage into a digital count. The number of counts is limited by the bit depth.
  • Every voltage in a range gets the same count. That's where stair-steps on smooth edges come from (you don't see them with enough pixels).

Low-frequency areas show problems

Smooth, flat areas (a plain wall, sky) change slowly, so too few bits shows as banding.

High-frequency areas hide problems

Busy detail (hair, lace, grass, fur) changes a lot from pixel to pixel, so a low bit depth is hard to notice.

  • Dithering mixes black and white dots to fake gray tones (newspaper style).
  • Why 8 bits if 5 looks OK? Editing a low-bit image shows artifacts right away. Fewer bits only make sense for small web or phone images where bandwidth matters.
Spotted on the slidesOne slide says "8 bits – 265 levels per channel". That's a typo: 2⁸ = 256. Write 256 on the exam.

A bitonal image is just 0s and 1s

1 bit per pixel: 0 = black, 1 = white. Fine for black-and-white text, tiny files.

Gray levels per number of bits

Each extra bit doubles the levels, so the bars barely show until about 12 bits, then they explode: 4,096 → 65,536.

Where you notice too few bits

Low frequency (smooth wall): bands show

High frequency (grass, fur): steps hide

04

Histograms: check all four

Luminance + red, green and blue

Cameras show a luminance (composite) histogram, and often the separate R, G and B histograms. The in-camera histogram is based on the JPEG, even when you shoot raw.

📊Look at all of them. The combined brightness can look fine while one color channel is clipped.

Expose to the right (her slide)

  • Applies specifically to digital raw capture
  • Base proper exposure on the highlights
  • Get the histogram snug against the right (255) without going over
  • It may look bright, but that's solved in editing
  • A raw sensor has a linear response (not true for JPEG): less visible noise in the highlights, more in the shadows

The Bayer pattern: where it came from

Kodak, 1976

  • Invented by Bryce Bayer of Kodak (patent 1976). Say "BUY-er", not "bear".
  • A color filter array (CFA) of red, green and blue filters over the photosites, with twice as many green to match the eye's sensitivity.
  • Won an Emmy in 2001 (National Academy of Television Arts and Sciences).
  • Each filter throws away about 2/3 of the light, so 2/3 of every color image is made-up data → demosaicing (see the Sep 28 tab).
Kodak storyKodak led early digital imaging but held onto film (about 800% markup). Its sensor division even made Leica's sensors.

01

Bayer pattern & demosaicing

Each photosite sees only one color

A sensor only measures brightness. To get color, a Bayer filter (color filter array, CFA) puts a red, green or blue filter over each photosite: 50% green, 25% red, 25% blue. So every pixel is missing two thirds of its color data. Demosaicing is the processing that fills in the missing colors from the neighbors.

How the standard method works

  • Green first: most data (50%) and where we're most sensitive, so it builds the luminance (brightness) image.
  • Then red and blue become the chrominance (color) images. Blue gets the biggest boost, so blue has the most noise.
  • Good algorithms look for edges and weight pixels along the edge more.

Why white needs all three

On white areas, the red, green and blue photosites are all "on". White = all colors of light together.

Armenian: demosaicing ≈ գույների վերականգնում (rebuilding colors), photosite = լուսազգայուն բջիջ.

02

Other color filter arrays

Bayer isn't the only pattern

No filter Foveon X3 (Sigma)

  • A direct image sensor: records red, green and blue at every photosite, using how deep each color goes into the silicon. Like color film's 3 layers.
  • Pros: no demosaicing (no interpolation), sharper still lifes, less processing.
  • Cons: blur on moving subjects, and "funky" colors under some lights.
  • Sigma bought Foveon in 2008, and it's the only company using it. No camera currently in production.
03

ISO is gain, and noise is not grain

You can't change how sensitive the sensor is

The photodiode's sensitivity is fixed. Raising ISO just amplifies the signal (more gain), and the noise gets amplified with it.

Low ISO

Longer exposure time for the same light, less noise.

High ISO

Shorter exposure time, more noise. Having a high ISO available doesn't mean you'll like using it.

Film Grain

Real silver halide crystals (or dye particles). Faster film = bigger crystals = a bigger target for photons = visible grain.

Digital Noise

Electronic noise. It has nothing to do with crystals. Don't call digital noise "grain".

Beyond the lectureThe professor says ISO = "International Standards Organization", so use her wording on the exam. The official English name is the International Organization for Standardization, and "ISO" comes from Greek isos = equal.
04

Three types of noise

Noise = a mismatch between the light that hits the sensor and the electronic signal

Her slide quotes Galer & Horvat (Digital Imaging Essential Skills): noise comes from random electrical activity on the sensor, and the lower the light, the greater the inconsistency.

Most common

Photon noise

Light itself isn't perfectly even. Photons arrive randomly, so the input is already noisy.

Like rain: even steady rain doesn't drop the same number of drops in every cup.

Heat

Thermal noise

Heat from the camera's electronics makes the photosite "see" fake photons. Fix: cooled sensors (used for IR and science cameras).

Hotter camera, long exposures → more thermal noise.

Readout

Transfer noise

Happens during readout: some charge gets left behind, or neighboring photosites interfere (like blooming).

Like pouring syrup from a can: you need a scraper to get the rest.

💡P-T-T: "Please Turn on the Tripod." Photon, Thermal, Transfer. Photon noise comes from the light, thermal from heat, transfer from readout.
05

Signal-to-noise ratio

How strong the signal is compared to the noise

SNR = 20 × log₁₀(Mean ÷ Std Dev)
  • Higher SNR = better (cleaner). A low standard deviation is what you want: less spread around the mean.
  • Units are dB (decibels: small d, capital B), borrowed from audio. On a lab answer the dB is often left off, but it is a dB.
  • SNR doesn't depend on amplification: if the mean and std dev are both multiplied by the same gain, the ratio stays the same.

For practice with real patches and graphs, open the Noise & SNR Lab and Patch Meter tiles.

06

Sensor size

Bigger photosite = bigger target for photons

  • Larger photosite → more light-gathering ability → less amplification needed → less noise. Crop sensors tend to have more noise.
  • More photosites (a larger array) → higher resolution.
  • A bigger sensor needs bigger, heavier, pricier lenses, and only a few full-frame sensors fit on one silicon wafer, so they cost more.
  • Pixel density is not tied to ISO.
  • Her target users: full frame → professionals; APS-C → professionals and "prosumers"; Four Thirds → "???"; anything smaller → consumer.
🏓Ping-pong ball test: throw it at a small bucket or a big barrel. You hit the barrel more often. Bigger photosite = more photons caught.
Beyond the lectureIn class, Four Thirds was called "about 30–40% smaller" than full frame. Measured by area it's actually about 74% smaller (crop factor 2 → ¼ the area). If the exam asks, use the class answer, but now you know the real math: area shrinks by the crop factor squared.
07

Expose to the right (ETTR)

Move dark tones up, away from the noise

A raw sensor responds linearly: the brightest stop gets half of all the levels, the next stop half of what's left, and so on. Shadows get very few levels and sit close to the noise. ETTR means exposing brighter so the histogram moves right (without clipping), then darkening it again in post.

Shadow levels: –Clipping: –

Stop (12-bit raw)Levels

✅ Works for

Low-key and mid-to-dark scenes that don't fill the whole dynamic range.

❌ Not for

Scenes with bright highlights and dark shadows: a white cat and a black cat, a bride and a groom, a bright day with clouds. The bright parts would clip.

📸Rules: shoot raw (JPEGs aren't linear and throw data away), watch the luminance histogram, and never clip what matters.
08

HDR (high dynamic range)

When the scene is too contrasty for one shot

  • Camera on a sturdy tripod (if it moves, the merge fails). Shoot several exposures: one for the highlights, one for the shadows, maybe more in between.
  • Merge in Photoshop or Lightroom (or dedicated tools). Don't "overbake" it.
  • Used for: architecture and real estate (a room plus the view through the windows), night venues with bright lights, sunny scenes with deep shadows.
⚖️ETTR vs HDR: ETTR = one shot, for scenes that fit the sensor. HDR = many shots, for scenes that don't fit.

The smallest pieces of digital memory

Raster vs vector

Pixels vs math

Raster: the pixels just get bigger

Vector: redrawn from math, always sharp

Raster

  • Image = a grid of pixel values (samples).
  • Fixed size, so resolution matters: a small file can't be enlarged much.
  • Used for photographs.

Vector

  • Shapes, lines and curves stored as geometry (instructions).
  • Scales to any size without changing.
  • Logos, illustrations, diagrams, typography, graphs. Example: EPS (Encapsulated PostScript).

Why is RAW less sharp?

Many cameras have an anti-aliasing filter, which is really a blur filter. It softens edges to prevent aliasing (stair-stepping), and processing sharpens the image back. Some very high-resolution cameras leave the filter out.

Which file is bigger: RAW or demosaiced?

The demosaiced image. RAW has 1 channel (one gray value per photosite), and a demosaiced image has 3 channels (red, green and blue at every pixel).

Raw processing may include (her slide)

Black-level correction · bad-pixel correction · white-level normalization · demosaicing · noise reduction · white balance · color correction · tone mapping · color-space encoding · sharpening · output encoding. The exact sequence varies among cameras and software.

Default slider settings come from psychophysics studies: experts and regular people adjust many images, and the average becomes the preset.

  • Bit depth = how many levels are available to describe the tones.
  • Dynamic range = the range between the smallest and largest useful values: max − min, or from the brightest detailed highlight to the darkest detailed shadow (a specular highlight isn't useful detail).
  • Printing is done at 8 bits because the eye can't tell more levels apart. Edit at more bits to avoid artifacts.
07

Non-destructive editing & the .xmp sidecar

The original pixels never change

Non-destructive means the original image data stays available, and edits are stored separately as instructions. The image is rendered when it's displayed or exported.

Her slide lists: raw adjustments · XMP instructions · catalog adjustments · virtual copies · adjustment layers · masks.

Raw adjustments

Exposure, contrast, color, noise reduction, sharpening, crop and masks are saved as instructions.

Catalog & virtual copies

Lightroom keeps instructions in its catalog. A virtual copy = another set of instructions on the same file.

Photoshop layers

Turn a layer off and the base image is untouched.

.xmp sidecar file

Created next to a proprietary RAW when you edit in Adobe Camera Raw. It holds the metadata and edit instructions. Delete it and your edits are gone. Always keep the RAW and its .xmp together.

💾Back up! A senior lost three months of capstone work to one failed hard drive with no backup. Remember 3-2-1 from Exam 1.
08

DNG (Digital Negative)

Adobe's open raw format

  • Introduced by Adobe in 2004, based on the TIFF 6.0 structure, current spec 1.7.1, publicly documented and open (royalty-free). Only a few cameras write it natively.
  • Legacy and versionable: Adobe guarantees an old DNG will still open years from now. No camera maker promises that for their own raw format.
  • About 10–15% smaller than the original raw, and works best in Adobe software.
  • After edits, a DNG updates its embedded preview. The original raw keeps the preview from capture.
  • Makers keep their "secret sauce" in private maker notes.

✅ Advantages

Public spec · broad software support · one consistent format · archival · simpler workflow

⚖️ Trade-offs

Converting takes time · maker-specific info may not transfer identically · you may still want the original raw · size depends on the settings

Convert with the Adobe DNG Converter (it can batch a whole folder), Lightroom or Bridge.

Archiving isn't just a file format

Should you convert to DNG?

preservation = redundancy + documentation + validation + migration plan

Redundancy

Several copies, for example a RAID (redundant array of independent disks). Put it behind a surge protector.

Documentation & validation

A README with your naming convention. Run a checksum to prove each copy matches.

Migration plan

Move files off dying media before you can't read them (floppies, old disks, CDs).

DNG today: version 1.7.1. Adobe keeps the DNG SDK (software development kit) available. DNG was originally lossless, but lossy DNG has been possible since Lightroom 4, and DNG is popular on smartphones with raw capture, and it defeats much of the point of raw.

02

The processing chain

From photons to a file

  • White balance in raw is read from the metadata and applied, not baked in. Shot under fluorescent with the camera set to tungsten? Fix it in Camera Raw with no loss.
  • Tone mapping ≈ the gamma curve. A sensor is linear (twice the photons = twice the signal), which is good for science. Our vision isn't linear, so photos get a tone curve to look natural.
  • You can render one raw many ways: TIFF, JPEG, HEIF, PSD…
03

TIFF

Tagged Image File Format

  • Created by Aldus in 1986, owned by Adobe since 1994. Originally made for scanners and desktop publishing.
  • Flexible raster format: grayscale, RGB, CMYK, CIELAB, indexed color; 8, 16 or 32 bits per channel; layers; alpha channels.
  • Compression: none, lossless, or lossy, your choice.
  • Classic TIFF uses 32-bit offsets, so the max is 4 GB. BigTIFF uses 64-bit offsets for bigger files (scientific and medical images).
Beyond the lectureIn class: Adobe charges a licensing fee, so not every program offers TIFF (use that on the exam). For reference, the TIFF 6.0 specification itself is published openly.

JPEG

Joint Photographic Experts Group

★ Her test pick · Test question (the chart)
TIFF vs JPEG: which is better for repeated editing, and which allows transparency?
TIFF for both. JPEG degrades every save and has no transparency.
  • JPEG is both a file format and a lossy compression method. Lossy = data thrown away is gone.
  • 8 bits per channel only. Can embed a color profile. Small files (about 500 KB – 8 MB). .jpg = .jpeg. Designed for efficient distribution.
  • Generational degradation: every re-save compresses an already compressed file, and artifacts pile up fast. Editing a JPEG? Save it as TIFF or PSD first.
  • In-camera JPEG: white balance, tone, color, sharpening, noise reduction and compression are baked in. Get it right in camera (custom white balance, User 1/User 2 settings).

06

How compression works

Images are full of redundancy

Lossless

Smaller file, and decompressing gives back the exact original. Nothing wrong with it.

Lossy

Throws away data it thinks you won't see. You get back an approximation, not the original.

Statistical

Repeated values: store "red ×4, blue ×7, green ×2" instead of 13 separate pixels.

Interpixel / spatial

Neighbors are similar. Store 100 and then the differences: 100, +1, 0, +2, +1, 0. Lossy: just call them all 100.

Psychovisual

We're easy to fool. Drop what the eye barely sees: fine detail, small color changes, contrast we can't notice.

How a JPEG is made

Y = brightness, Cb = blue–yellow, Cr = red–green. We see brightness much better than color, so cutting the color channels in half already saves lots of space.

07

Compression artifacts

Use the right words

Compression changes FILE SIZE, not RESOLUTION

Example: 600 × 800 px TIFF = 1.5 MB. Same 600 × 800 px JPEG = 119 KB.

Blocking

The 8 × 8 blocks become visible because variation inside each block was squeezed out.

Posterization / banding

Smooth tones become steps because there are too few tonal levels. (Class: "exclusively a JPEG artifact".)

Ringing & detail loss

Halos next to edges, fine texture smoothed away, color errors, soft edges.

Aliasing

A sampling problem: detail too fine for the pixels gives moiré, false detail and color artifacts. Not only JPEG: downsampling causes it too.

Stair-stepping

Diagonal edges turn into steps. Say "stair-stepping", not "jaggies" (sounds amateur).

Pixelation = NOT an artifact

Zooming to 3200% just magnifies pixels. That's magnification, not damage.

Beyond the lectureOn the exam, use "posterization is a JPEG artifact". In practice, banding can also appear when an 8-bit image is pushed hard in editing. That's one more reason to edit at 16 bits.

See aliasing (moiré) happen

Fine rings sampled with enough pixels look right. Lower the pixels and false patterns (moiré) appear that aren't really there.

03

Pinhole vs lens

Without focusing, every pixel gets the same light, so there's no image

Pinhole

  • Can be very sharp, but only at one exact image distance for that pinhole size.
  • Tiny opening, so very long exposures.
  • See pinhole.org.

Lens

  • Collects light much more efficiently.
  • Brings the image to a point of focus.
  • Lets you control depth of field.

The image is upside down and backwards

Every real lens image is inverted. A DSLR flips it back with a mirror and pentaprism. A mirrorless camera flips it in software. A 4×5 camera's ground glass shows it upside down and backwards, so to move the image left you move right.

Pinhole camera: size of the hole

How a DSLR shows the image right-side up

The lens image is upside down and backwards. The mirror sends it up, and the pentaprism flips it right-side up for the optical viewfinder. When you press the shutter, the mirror swings up and light reaches the sensor. Mirrorless cameras flip the image in software instead.

04

Positive vs negative lenses

Converge or diverge

★ Her test pick · Test question
Positive vs negative lens: what does each one do?
Positive (convex): thicker in the center, converges light, forms a real image.
Negative (concave): thinner in the center, diverges light, can't form a real image alone.

➕ Positive (convex)

  • Thicker in the center than at the edges, with at least one convex (outward-curved) side.
  • Makes light converge and forms a real image by itself. It can start a fire with sunlight.
  • Types: double convex, plano-convex (one flat side).

➖ Negative (concave)

  • Thinner in the center, with concave sides.
  • Makes light diverge and cannot form a real image alone.
  • Used with positive lenses. Every camera lens has some, for example the achromatic doublet (a positive and a negative cemented together).

The green ray goes through the optical axis (the center of the lens) undeviated. Rays parallel to it come "from infinity". In the lab, a table's length is far enough.

05

u, v, f and nodal points

The vocabulary of a lens

1/f = 1/u + 1/v
  • u = object distance (subject → lens). v = image distance (lens → image). The focal plane is where the sensor goes.
  • Focal length = the distance from the back nodal point to the focal plane when the object is at infinity.
  • Parallel rays meet at the principal focal point. Light through the front gives the back focal point. Turn the lens around to find the front one.
  • Nodal points: a ray that leaves the lens parallel to how it entered; extend it to the axis to find the front and back nodal points. In a thin simple lens they sit together at the center. In compound lenses they're apart.
🔤U → O: close the top of the U and it becomes an O for Object distance. That leaves V for image distance.

Nodal slide (lab)

Focus a distant bare bulb, then pivot the lens slightly. If the point of light doesn't move, you're on the nodal point, so measure the focal length. If it moves, slide the lens and try again.

Why it matters: for panoramas, rotate the camera on the lens's nodal point (panoramic head) for less distortion when stitching.

Not on the testTwo lenses touching: 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂. With air between them it gets more complicated.

Nodal points: in parallel, out parallel

A ray aimed at the front nodal point leaves as if from the back nodal point, parallel to how it came in. On her nodal slide: pivot the lens, and if the image doesn't move, you're on the nodal point.

Measuring focal length: convex, concave, mirror

F = principal focal point, where parallel rays come into focus. For a negative lens you trace the spreading rays backward. Concave mirrors are used in catadioptric lenses.

    07

    Refraction

    Without it, lenses wouldn't work

    • Refraction = light bending as it passes from one medium into another (unless it hits perpendicular, like on the optical axis).
    • The refractive index says how much a material bends light. Water's is higher than air's, so a straw looks broken.
    • Glass bends light, so lenses can focus. Different wavelengths bend differently (chromatic aberration, next lecture).
    • Short focal length → rays bend sharply → more distortion (wide angle). Long focal length → gentle bending → more magnification, less distortion.

      04

      Telephoto vs reverse telephoto (retrofocus)

      "A very good test question"

      ★ Her test pick · “A very good test question”
      Telephoto vs reverse telephoto (retrofocus): how are the elements arranged?
      Telephoto: positive element in front, negative behind → rear nodal point falls in front of the lens.
      Retrofocus: negative in front, positive behind → rear nodal point falls behind the lens, so a wide angle clears the DSLR mirror.

      Normal telephoto

      Positive element in front, negative behind. The back nodal point falls in front of the lens, so a long focal length fits in a shorter barrel. The lens sits close to the sensor and mirror.

      Reverse telephoto = retrofocus

      Elements swapped: negative in front, positive behind. The back nodal point moves behind the lens group, so the lens sits farther from the mirror and doesn't hit it. Her slide calls it the "Reverse Telephoto Wide Angle Lens".

      Beyond the lectureRetrofocus is how wide-angle lenses for DSLRs are built. A plain 20 mm lens would have to sit 20 mm from the sensor, inside the mirror's swing. Retrofocus gives a short focal length with a long back-focus distance. Telephotos do the opposite: a long focal length in a short lens.

      Framing vs perspective

      She "can't impose upon that enough"

      ★ Her test pick · She “can't impose upon that enough”
      What does changing the focal length change, and what does moving the camera change?
      Focal length → framing.
      Camera position → perspective.
      Change the LENS → change FRAMING
      Move the CAMERA → change PERSPECTIVE
      • Perspective = the relative size of objects in a scene, and it's controlled by camera position (distance).
      • Move closer and foreground objects become relatively larger. Everything at the same distance scales together.
      • Why people think lenses change perspective: with a telephoto you usually stand far away, and with a wide angle close. The distance is what changes the look.
      06

      Prime vs zoom

      One focal length or many

      Prime

      Fixed focal length, often a large maximum aperture ("fast"), optimized for that one focal length.

      Zoom (variable focal length)

      Moves lens groups inside to change the angle of view. Flexible framing, no lens swaps, but a more complex optical design. It may be less well corrected for aberrations, slower (smaller max aperture), and less sharp than a prime.

      Her slide: image quality, maximum aperture, size and cost depend on the specific lens design.

      07

      Macro lenses & reproduction ratio

      True macro = 1:1 or more

      • A macro lens focuses at very short distances for high magnification, and it's built to minimize distortion and correct flatness of field up close.
      • 1:1 (life size): a 15 mm ladybug is 15 mm on the sensor. Only lenses that reach 1:1 or more are true macro lenses.
      • 1:2: half life size. 2:1: twice life size.
      • The macro scale on the lens barrel shows 1:1, 1:2 and so on.
      08

      Supplemental lenses & diopters

      A cheap way to get closer

      D = 1 / f (f in METERS) · f = 1 / D
      diopters add: Dc = D + Ds

      Diopter calculator

      • A supplemental (close-up) lens screws onto the front of a lens. Its power is given in diopters, which are defined in meters.
      • Examples from her slide: 2 diopters → f = ½ m = 500 mm. 4 diopters → ¼ m = 250 mm.
      • A cheap, light option (great for travel), but its distortion correction is limited, and quality depends on the glass.
      • The number on the ring (e.g. 49 mm) is the thread diameter, not the focal length.
      • Lab: with one added you must move the camera closer to focus (her floor tiles are 1 ft each!).
      Beyond the lectureHer slide says a supplemental lens is a "cheap way of increasing focal length". Use her words on the exam. Strictly, a + diopter adds power, so the combined focal length gets shorter. That's what lets you focus closer, and the result is more magnification.
      09

      Specialty lenses

      Tilt-shift, mirror and soft focus

      Tilt-shift (perspective control)

      Shifts up and down or side to side to prevent converging lines (tall buildings leaning in). It's like a small piece of a 4×5 camera's movements, great for architecture and real estate. It can also fake "miniature" scenes in camera.

      Catadioptric (mirror, folded optics)

      Curved mirrors plus glass fold the light path, so you get a long focal length in a short, light lens (like telescopes). Drawbacks: usually one fixed f-stop (no depth-of-field control), the f-number ignores the light lost to the center mirror, so metering can be off, and you may need ND filters.

      Soft focus (portrait)

      Made by under-correcting spherical aberration. The softness is only around the edges; the center stays sharp. The effect shrinks as you stop down. It's not the same as defocusing (that's just blur).

      👁️Portraits: the eyes must be sharp. The rest can fall off.

      Tilt-shift: fix converging lines

      Catadioptric (mirror) lens: folded light path

      The dark secondary mirror blocks the center. That's why there's only one fixed f-stop, why the f-number doesn't account for the lost light, and why out-of-focus highlights look like little donuts.
