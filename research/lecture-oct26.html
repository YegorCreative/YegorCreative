01

Autofocus methods

Phase detection vs contrast detection

Phase detection

  • Calculates the distance to the object, then moves the lens straight to that distance (and checks once more).
  • Very fast, but needs a separate sensor or special microlenses.
  • If the lens is miscalibrated, focus can be off (front- or back-focusing).

Contrast detection

  • The lens moves through the focus range while the camera measures contrast.
  • Maximum contrast = in focus.
  • Slower, but cheaper and smaller because it uses the main sensor.

Watch them focus

Contrast is highest at perfect focus.

  • LensAlign (in the lab) checks whether your camera and lens are calibrated: shoot it, zoom in, and the zero mark should be sharp. If not, the camera front- or back-focuses. Some cameras let you adjust this, but ask Dan or Nanette first.
  • Manual focus: go back and forth past focus, then zero in. (Most students are out of practice because they rely on autofocus.)
  • Why turn autofocus off for the ND filter patches: a flat patch has no contrast or edges, so the lens just "hunts" back and forth. It won't break, but it's needless wear on a mechanical part.

How do we achieve perspective?

Showing a 3-D world on a 2-D sensor, print or screen

Perspective = the relationships between the objects we image: how we show a three-dimensional world on two-dimensional media (sensor, paper, screen). It applies to drawing and painting too.

CueHow it shows depth
Depth of fieldSharp subject, blurred background separates near from far
LightingLight and shadow give objects shape and volume
OverlapWhatever blocks something else is in front of it
Aerial hazeFaraway things look hazier and lower in contrast ("incredible" for depth)
ColorFar objects look cooler and less saturated
Stereophotography / holographyTwo views (or a hologram) give real 3-D: "a little bit of cheating"

03

Binocular vision

Two eyes, one 3-D picture

  • Binocular vision = using both eyes together to get a 3-D, stereoscopic view (length, width and depth). All animals with forward-facing eyes have it. A fish with eyes on the sides has no overlap, so no depth perception.
  • You see with your brain: the signals cross at the optic chiasm, go to the LGN and then to the visual cortex at the back of the head, which fuses both images into one.
  • Each eye sees from a slightly different spot. The brain triangulates the difference. Binocular disparity = the difference in where an object lands in the left vs right eye. Average pupil separation is 2.5 inches.
  • Disparity increases as objects get closer. At 450 ft the separation ratio is 2000:1: almost no disparity.
  • Binocular vision works well within about 18–20 ft. Beyond 20 ft the brain uses shadows and relative motion.
  • If the brain can't fuse the two images: binocular dysfunction, no depth perception (causes include blindness, differential focus, strabismus/lazy eye, processing problems). Depth perception develops in the toddler years.

Try it: disparity

Hold a finger out, close one eye, then the other, back and forth. The finger "jumps" against the background. That jump is binocular disparity.

Try it: dominant eye

With both eyes open, hide a far object behind your thumb. Close one eye, then the other. The eye where the thumb moves less is your dominant eye, probably the one you put to the viewfinder.

04

Linear perspective

Horizon line · vanishing point · orthogonal lines

Linear perspective = the geometric way of showing that objects get smaller and closer together as they get farther away. We judge distance by how things shrink and how lines converge. The object distance and the focal length are the critical factors.

Draw a one-point street

  1. Horizon line: runs across the scene, usually at the viewer's eye level. It can be where a wall meets the floor, not just sky and ground.
  2. Vanishing point: for one-point perspective, a point on the horizon (often near the center) where all the receding lines meet.
  3. Orthogonal lines: visual rays connecting the corners of shapes to the vanishing point. Draw them, build the shapes, then erase them.

Two-point perspective: two vanishing points, like standing on a street corner looking down both streets.

05

The two image-size rules

"Anything in blue will probably be on a test"

★ Her test pick · in blue on her slide
How do focal length and object distance change image size?
Image size is directly proportional to focal length.
Image size is inversely proportional to object distance.

Changes in perspective usually come from changing camera position (object distance) and focal length.

Her boot experiment

  • Object ratio 1:2 → size ratio 2:1. If the second object is twice as far away (10 ft, then 20 ft), it looks half as big. Her folding chairs: the far chair fits exactly in the gap between the near chair's seat and top.
  • Doubling the focal length doubles everything: her slide measured the boots at 0.75 and 0.375 in (50 mm), 1.5 and 0.75 in (100 mm), 3.0 and 1.5 in (200 mm). The ratio between them never changes, so focal length doesn't change perspective. Only moving does.
  • Look up forced perspective photography: it plays with exactly this.
